\subsection{GEN-001: Data Scaling at Fixed Optimizer Budget}
\recordstatus{Preliminary; single-seed visual evidence.}
\evidence{W\&B screenshots, including the comparison with ordinary LoRA supplied
2026-10-23. Raw metric exports and immutable run configurations are unavailable.}

\textbf{Question.} Does more training data improve validation agreement within
the same number of optimizer updates?

\textbf{Setup.} The direct generator arms use nested pools of 4K, 8K, and 16K
examples: one, two, or four subsets per domain across math, code, STEM, and chat.
All three run for 1,500 updates with seed 42. The documented recipe uses
\texttt{lk\_hybrid}, four episodes per update, training context sizes 1--8,
rank 32, alpha 64, and an 8,192-token microbatch budget. AdamW uses learning
rate $10^{-4}$, weight decay 0.01, gradient clipping 0.85, and 100 learning-rate
warmup updates. This scheduler warmup is distinct from bank reconstruction.
Validation uses 100 held-out rows per domain from subset 00000, grouped at
context size 4, every 250 updates including updates 0 and 1,500.
The additional curve is labeled \texttt{scale-1-lora-gseed42}; its intended
comparison is ordinary LoRA trained on the 4K pool at the same update budget.
Historical configuration artifacts are needed to verify all matched settings.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/GEN-001-scaling-with-lora-2026-10-23.png}
\caption{GEN-001: supplied training-loss and domain-validation plots. The three
direct generator arms use 4K, 8K, and 16K training examples with the same
1,500-update budget. Ordinary scale-1 LoRA is included as a training baseline.
Agreement is an offline validation metric, not measured serving acceptance.
Curves are visual evidence; no raw values or uncertainty estimates are supplied.}
\label{fig:scaling}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Observed behavior.} In Figure~\ref{fig:scaling}, generator validation
curves largely overlap across data scales. Most improvement occurs early, with
smaller gains thereafter. The ordinary LoRA curve improves more slowly and
finishes visibly below the generators on math, code, and STEM. Chat differences
are smaller, with a late dip in the scale-1 generator curve. Training loss is
noisy, while the generator trends finish below the ordinary LoRA trend.

\textbf{Working interpretation.} On this split, seed, and update budget, pool
size has a small observed effect compared with the gain from training itself.
Increasing available data fourfold does not produce a correspondingly large
validation gain in these curves. This is a fixed-update comparison: it neither
matches epochs nor guarantees equal processed tokens, compute, or wall time.
Larger pools receive less average reuse. The result therefore motivates asking
whether longer training or broader validation would reveal additional data
benefits, rather than concluding that data quantity is generally unimportant.

\textbf{Limits.} Validation rows are held out, but their subset identity is
represented in every training pool. One seed and screenshots cannot establish
statistical equivalence or a precise ranking. Generator versus LoRA differences
also include the much larger generator parameter count. These training curves
do not add an older generator row to the current serving benchmark tables.

\subsection{GEN-004: Bank Warmup and the Direct-Training Decision}
\recordstatus{Preliminary adaptation observation; bank workflow retired.}
\evidence{Adaptation screenshot supplied 2026-10-23; historical protocol recorded
in the 2026-10-07 working notes. Exact execution date and raw exports unavailable.}

\textbf{Question.} Does reconstructing previously trained bank LoRAs provide a
useful lasting advantage over training a fresh generator on the original data?

\textbf{Setup.} The historical study adapts four initializations on
\texttt{math-00001}: a bank-pretrained generator, a fresh generator, a transferred
ordinary LoRA, and a fresh ordinary LoRA. The recorded protocol uses seed 42,
last-token conditioning, training context sizes 1--8, and one adaptation epoch.
The supplied plot spans roughly 200 optimizer updates and evaluates context 8;
this is distinct from GEN-001's context-4 validation. Both generator arms are
optimized through the drafter loss during adaptation.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.70\textwidth]{assets/GEN-004-adaptation-2026-10-23.png}
\caption{GEN-004: supplied adaptation curves for pretrained/fresh generators and
transferred/fresh ordinary LoRAs. Bank pretraining improves the starting point,
but the fresh generator reaches a similar final context-8 validation loss.
The displayed comparison does not match total pretraining-plus-adaptation cost.}
\label{fig:adaptation}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Observed behavior.} The pretrained generator starts at a lower
validation loss than the fresh generator. By the end of the displayed adaptation,
the two generator validation curves meet at a similar loss. The transferred
ordinary LoRA changes little, while the fresh ordinary LoRA improves but remains
above the generators. Figure~\ref{fig:adaptation} therefore shows an initialization
benefit from bank warmup without an observed final-loss advantage at this budget.

\textbf{Design decision.} We retired the LoRA-bank approach as unhelpful for the
current workflow. We use the original examples directly to train the generator
with the drafting loss. Bank reconstruction first turns those examples into
trained LoRA factors and then asks the generator to reproduce that proxy.
Direct training optimizes the desired drafting objective itself and removes
both bank construction and proxy reconstruction from the workflow. The observed
fresh-generator catch-up supports choosing this simpler route; no further bank
experiments are part of the current plan.

\textbf{Scope of the claim.} Direct training is the chosen, more useful approach
for this project, rather than a demonstrated universal ordering of the methods.
This adaptation study is not a matched-total-budget comparison of using the same
examples for bank warmup versus direct pretraining. It also mixes conditioning
and parameter-capacity differences between generators and ordinary LoRA. Raw
metrics, repeated seeds, and complete historical provenance are missing.
We retain the new screenshot without reusing approximate endpoint values from
older screenshots in the working notes.

\subsection{BATCH-001: Shared-Adapter Concurrency Sweep}
\recordstatus{Measured summary supplied; preliminary, correctness unchecked.}
\evidence{112 measurements: four models, C1/C2/C4/C8, seven categories.
Detailed summary received 2026-10-24; C1 values reuse GEN-003.}

\textbf{Question and protocol.} Does one adapter conditioned on a group improve
acceptance and serving throughput as concurrency increases? All four arms use
fixed closed cohorts: vacant slots remain empty until the last request finishes.
The generator produces one shared adapter per cohort and merges it into the
frozen drafter, restoring the base afterward. Original EAGLE3 and static LoRA
use the same admission restriction. This is a matched-policy comparison, not a
benchmark of unrestricted native vLLM serving. Each measurement completes 50
requests; workload hashes match across all 16 variants per category.

\textbf{Budget and identity limits.} The static-LoRA recipe describes 4K examples
and 1,500 updates, while the generator labels describe 24K training. G2 denotes
\texttt{generator-24k-2000step}, mapped by the example configuration to
\texttt{epoch-0002}; an earlier epoch-2 archive recorded 2,676 updates. G4 denotes
\texttt{generator-4000step}. These labels do not independently verify exact
training progress, hashes or matched compute. Native request-level records,
hardware and launch/source provenance are unavailable.

\input{generated/shared_acceptance}

\textbf{Observed behavior.} Both generators exceed original EAGLE3 throughput
in every ID category at all four concurrencies. The G4 ID category-mean advantage
over static LoRA narrows to 1.08\% at C8, although its acceptance-length gains
there are 4.75/3.85/2.66/1.68\% for math/code/STEM/chat. OOD behavior is mixed:
at C8 G4 trails EAGLE3 by 4.68\% on HumanEval, and trails static LoRA by 1.30\%
on math reasoning and 2.64\% on summarization. An OOD mean near zero does not
establish equivalence. Full ID/OOD throughput rows appear in the appendix.

\textbf{Latency and execution counters.} Recorded generator/merge/restoration
GPU time accounts for only 0.070--0.105\% of measurement wall time across the
generator arms. It excludes uninstrumented CPU work and other synchronization.
Adapter records contain 25/13/7 completed cohorts for 50 requests at C2/C4/C8,
the minimum possible counts; this does not reveal vacant-slot time within each
cohort. C8 mean cohort size is $50/7=7.143$.

\input{generated/shared_ttft}

G4 chat median TTFT rises from 49.6 ms at C1 to 1,447.6 ms at C8 while aggregate
throughput increases. The 2--3 ms generator invocation alone cannot account
for that first-token delay. Queueing, cohort completion and prefill are not
separated in the summary. HumanEval at C8 also illustrates why acceptance alone
is insufficient: G4 acceptance length is 2.579 versus EAGLE3's 2.541, despite
lower throughput.

\textbf{Working assessment.} Useful ID adaptation is observed under closed
cohorts, but a strong incremental deployment benefit from our added sharing
and admission policy is not established. Ordinary GPU batching itself remains
useful: most C8/C1 scaling is also available to the original drafter. Comparing
within one category and concurrency is essential. Reused C1 values and LoRA
startup retries are not independent repeats or controls for runtime drift.

\subsection{BATCH-002: Conditioning Size at Matched Data and Updates}
\recordstatus{Partially measured; supplied C8 throughput only.}
\evidence{Table received 2026-10-25 for static LoRA and separately trained
K1/K2/K4 generators. K8 is unreported; native acceptance/latency unavailable.}

\textbf{Question and intended setup.} Does conditioning on more prompt summaries
improve a generator at a matched 24K-example, 1,000-update budget? Four separate
generators are configured for $K=1,2,4,8$, alongside a static LoRA. Every episode
uses eight loss examples and selects $K$ of their summaries reproducibly;
all eight still contribute loss. Four episodes per update yield 32,000 example
presentations per arm, rather than 1,000 updates split between arms. Intended
shared settings are seed 42, rank 32/alpha 64 V/O adapters, \texttt{lk\_hybrid},
AdamW LR $10^{-4}$, weight decay 0.01, clipping 0.85, 100-update LR warmup and an
8,192-token microbatch budget. Architecture and initial generator weights match.
Effective run provenance is still missing.

\input{generated/conditioning}

Serving holds concurrency at eight with one shared adapter per closed cohort,
conditioned on $\min(K,\text{actual cohort size})$ summaries. Training K and
serving K change together across separately trained models; this is not an
inference-only ablation of one checkpoint. The intended export has 64 held-out
prompts per category, 256-token caps and three warmups, but the supplied table
alone cannot verify effective request counts or workload identities.

\textbf{Observed behavior and limits.} All three reported generators exceed
matched-budget static LoRA on all four ID categories. K2 leads math, K1 leads
code/STEM, and K4 leads chat. Larger K does not produce monotonically higher
throughput. Missing K8, acceptance, actual conditioning counts and repeats
prevent a complete ranking or a claim that larger conditioning sets are
intrinsically unhelpful. Combined with BATCH-001, this supports the engineering
assessment that the current fixed-cohort sharing technique has limited observed
incremental benefit; it does not establish that GPU batching is ineffective.

\subsection{BATCH-003: Per-Request Adapters with Continuous Admission}
\recordstatus{Measured C1/C2/C4 summaries; correctness unchecked.}
\evidence{24 supplied measurements received 2026-10-25, two arms and four ID
categories. C8 and complete runtime provenance are absent.}

\textbf{Question and protocol.} Can each request retain its own generated LoRA
while the native scheduler continuously admits and retires requests? The intended
generator checkpoint is \texttt{step-00004000} of
\texttt{azeeer/eagle3-generator-all24k-10ep-v1}. Each request conditions once on
its own prompt summary. Draft-only native multi-LoRA executes request-specific
V/O factors; no shared weight merge is performed. The intended baseline is
original EAGLE3 with original vLLM sources and native continuous admission.
BF16, eager V1 execution and other documented runtime restrictions still apply;
this is not a fully default-tuned vLLM comparison. Supplied labels do not verify
imported source hashes, exact weights or hardware.

All matched pairs share workload hashes and complete 50 requests. Generator,
preparation and completion counters each equal 50, with one conditioning example
and one request per adapter. Mean actual draft batch sizes are about 1.95--1.96
at C2 and 3.77--3.84 at C4. These counters support concurrent execution of the
path but do not independently establish per-request semantic isolation.

\input{generated/per_request_rates}

\textbf{Acceptance versus throughput.} Per-request acceptance exceeds native
EAGLE3 throughout the reported ID grid. At C4, acceptance-length gains are
22.24/14.14/13.09/5.93\% for math/code/STEM/chat. Throughput improves 5--9\% on
math, stays close on code/STEM and regresses 5--9\% on chat. The proposal-quality
hypothesis holds descriptively; broad serving acceleration does not.
Per-request and earlier shared-generator acceptance rates differ by less than
0.8 percentage points in every matched category/concurrency cell. That is a
cross-study observation, not evidence of equivalence: scheduling, adapter
execution, conditioning policy and unverified runtime conditions differ.

\input{generated/per_request_ttft}

\textbf{Systems diagnosis still open.} Per-request median TTFT is higher than
native EAGLE3 by roughly 10--18 ms. One-time generator work costs 3.12--3.30 ms
per request and loading costs 1.07--1.13 ms; their recorded sum is only
0.098--0.419\% of category wall time. Recurring mapping and unmerged projection
work are plausible costs, not yet measured causes. Although workload hashes
also match BATCH-001 and C4 TTFT is much lower than the old closed-cohort G4
values, the change cannot be assigned solely to continuous admission. Some
output-token totals differ slightly; matching prompts do not establish equal
outputs. Retain native continuous admission while profiling adapter execution
separately. No task-accuracy or verified-losslessness claim follows.

\textbf{Observed geometry.} Overall dynamism increases from 0.3424 to 0.5158.
Chat/code/math increase monotonically; reading dips at rank 16 before increasing.
Math has the largest reported dynamism at every rank. Cosine similarity
\emph{decreases} both within and between categories, with a larger fall between

The reported \texttt{eff\_dim} and \texttt{erank} are retained as distinct labels,
without assigning unprovided formulas or units.

\textbf{Observed serving behavior.} All ranks exceed the displayed 53.53\%
acceptance and 36.1 tokens/s reference. Rank 8 to 64 adds 1.77 acceptance
percentage points and 1.2 tokens/s (3.02\% relative to rank 8). Successive
acceptance increments are 0.70/0.59/0.48 pp, suggesting diminishing increments
in this series. Small adjacent gaps have no repeat uncertainty. The original
plots have interrupted vertical axes; numeric tables preserve absolute values.

\textbf{Interpretation and next evidence.} Higher rank accompanies more varied,
more category-separated adapters and higher reported acceptance/token rate.
This association does not establish that adapter dynamism causes the serving
improvement. Unknown acceptance denominators and throughput aggregation prevent
pooling these figures with BATCH-001/003. A matched static-LoRA rank sweep,
resource costs, original metric script and checkpoint-linked repeated runs would
be needed to isolate conditional benefits or choose an optimal rank.
